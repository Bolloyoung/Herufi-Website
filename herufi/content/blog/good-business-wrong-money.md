A company can be a good business and a bad investment at the same time, for the same investor. That is not a paradox, and it is not a polite way of saying the company is mediocre. It is the single most useful idea in African agricultural finance, and a decade of disappointment in the sector comes from missing it.

Consider a maize processor with a 12 per cent margin, growing revenue 40 per cent a year, with contracts from two large buyers and a waiting list of farmers who want to supply it. It is an excellent borrower. It is a poor venture investment. Both of those are true at once, and neither is a judgement about the quality of the company.

The previous article established that small farm loans fail on cost rather than on risk: expected credit losses barely move across loan sizes while the cost of making a loan moves 39 times as much. That means the businesses are not uniquely dangerous. It leaves the obvious question unanswered.

**If these businesses are not dangerous, what exactly is wrong with the money they have been offered?** The short answer is that it did not match them. Five very different kinds of agricultural company have been handed broadly one kind of capital, and only one of the five was ever built to take it.

---

## Equity left agricultural technology faster than it left anything else

Two facts from 2025 look contradictory until they sit side by side.

African technology funding went up. The continent's startups raised 4.1 billion dollars, about a quarter more than the year before. Debt reached a record 1.6 billion, which was 41 per cent of all capital deployed, against 31 per cent in 2024 and 17 per cent in 2019.

African agricultural technology went down. The sector raised 168 million dollars in 2025 against 207 million in 2024. 2024 had itself brought the first annual decline since anyone started tracking the sector, so 2025 was the second fall in a row. Inside that total, equity dropped from roughly 328 million dollars in 2022 to roughly 80 million in 2025. For the first time, equity was a minority of what the sector raised.

Two things about that data deserve stating plainly. The 2023 figure is not disclosed in the coverage available, so the decline is measured across a gap rather than year by year. And a second tracker, Partech, puts 2025 agricultural technology equity at 93 million dollars rather than 80, because the two use different definitions of the sector. The direction is not in dispute. The decimal places are.

So the market rose while agriculture fell, and within agriculture the part that collapsed was specifically equity. The usual reading is that investors got burned and walked away. That is at most half of it. The more useful reading is that capital is doing something rational and slightly embarrassing. It is discovering, late, that it had been offering these companies the wrong instrument for a decade.

## Venture capital is not patient money. It is a product with requirements.

To see why the instrument was wrong, it helps to be precise about what venture capital actually needs, because "risk appetite" is not the answer.

A venture fund needs a small number of enormous winners. It is paid for by its two or three best investments, not by its average one. Most of its companies return little or nothing, so the ones that work must return very large multiples. It needs those winners to turn into cash within roughly ten years, because a fund has a life and at the end of it the investors want their money back. And it needs growth that outruns cost: a business that can add a thousand customers without adding a thousand customers' worth of expense.

Now hold a typical African agricultural business against that list.

A maize processor earns a margin on every tonne it handles, usually somewhere between 5 and 20 per cent. To double its revenue it must buy twice as much maize, hire more people, rent more storage and tie up more cash. Revenue grows, and everything required to support revenue grows with it. Its exit options are thin: if things go well it may be bought by a larger agribusiness, and it will almost certainly never list on an exchange. And its risks are mostly not the ones a founder can fix, because weather, commodity prices and whether the buyer pays on time sit outside anyone's control.

None of that makes it a bad company. It makes it a bad fit for one specific instrument, which is an entirely different judgement. The sector has spent years treating the second as a verdict on the company rather than on the money.

## Five business models, five balance sheets, one cheque

The deeper problem is that agricultural technology is not one industry. It is at least five, with five different financial shapes. Each one below carries a real company, because a framework nobody can check against the record is just a diagram.

A word first on what those cases can and cannot show. Naming companies is easy and unfair if it is done carelessly, so each case states what was raised, what happened and when, with a source at the foot of this article. None of these businesses succeeded or failed for one reason, and nothing here claims the instrument was the only cause. What the cases show is a pattern consistent with the argument. One limit is worth stating plainly: none of these companies publishes audited profit figures. So "doing well" below means raising capital the balance sheet can actually service, not demonstrated profitability.

**The input distributor** sells seed and fertiliser on a thin margin, and its money sits in stock. Goods go out before planting and cash comes back after harvest, once or twice a year. What fits is a revolving credit line in local currency that expands before the season and shrinks after it. What it often got was growth equity priced as though inventory turning twice a year behaved like software.

*The case.* iProcure built the largest agricultural input distribution network in Kenya by reach, serving more than a million farmers. It raised about 17 million dollars across its life, a mix of debt and equity. In April 2024 it entered administration after failing to close a new round, and KPMG was appointed administrator. The reported unpaid debts were small against what it had raised, perhaps 1.5 million dollars. A business whose money sits in depots and stock needs a facility that breathes with the season. What it had was investor rounds, and a round that does not arrive is not a facility.

**The crop buyer and processor** buys produce, adds a margin by processing it, and sells on. What it owns is machinery, stock in a warehouse and contracts with buyers, and its cash is bunched around harvest. What fits is trade finance against the stock, warehouse receipt finance where the system allows it, and long term debt for the plant itself. What it often got was venture equity expecting growth that tonnage cannot produce.

*The case.* Twiga Foods raised more than 180 million dollars, more than any other African agricultural technology company, and built its own trucks, depots and a logistics hub. Then it spent three years taking that apart. It cut 283 jobs in 2023 and 59 more in 2024. In 2025 it created a holding company, cut over 300 roles, and announced a shift to an asset light distribution model, retreating from the capital heavy approach it had been funded to build. In August 2026 a Twiga subsidiary entered administration. The pivot to asset light is the part worth sitting with. It is the company agreeing, after 180 million dollars, that the balance sheet and the cheque never matched.

**The lender embedded in the supply chain** lends to farmers it already trades with, so its loan book is the business. What fits is a debt facility to fund the loan book, loss cover on the portfolio, and equity only for the technology that finds and assesses borrowers. What it often got was equity funding the loan book itself. That is the most expensive mistake on this list, because a loan book is the one thing in agriculture that makes genuinely good collateral. Funding it with equity throws that away.

*The case, and this one is the contrast.* In May 2026 Apollo Agriculture completed Kenya's first private agriculture focused securitisation. In plain terms, it bundled the loans it had made to farmers and sold investors a security backed by the repayments. That raised 276 million shillings, about 2.1 million dollars, against a pool of 370 million shillings covering 23,839 farmers. IDH Farmfit Fund anchored it and Kaleidofin structured it, and it is the first tranche of a programme targeting 2.37 billion shillings. Read the structure rather than the size. The loan book is funded by money secured on the loan book, in the same currency the farmers repay in, and the equity is left to pay for the technology. That is the whole argument of this article, executed.

**The digital advisory service or marketplace** sells software, advice or access, and can add customers without adding much cost. What fits is venture capital. This is the one case where it genuinely does.

*The case.* Pula sells agricultural insurance, and almost none of it directly. It works through more than a hundred partners: banks, governments, input suppliers. Somebody else was already reaching the farmer, so Pula does not pay to reach them. It raised 20 million dollars in April 2024 and says it has covered more than 15 million farmers.

Now notice who wrote that cheque. BlueOrchard led it, with the International Finance Corporation and a World Bank managed fund alongside. So even the one model with genuinely venture shaped economics was funded here by impact and development investors, not by classic venture funds. That is worth pausing on before anyone concludes the answer is simply more venture capital.

**The asset financier** finances tractors, irrigation and equipment that earn money over several years, with the asset itself as security. What fits is long term local currency debt, and eventually the ability to bundle and refinance the portfolio once there is a track record. What it often got was a seven year fund backing a ten year asset, which is a mismatch dressed up as an investment.

*The case.* SunCulture sells solar irrigation pumps to farmers on credit, which means its growth is limited by how fast it can finance what customers owe it. In September 2026 it closed a 10 million dollar senior secured facility from the Mirova Gigaton Fund into a special purpose vehicle that buys those receivables, structured by Kaleidofin. A year earlier it had announced a 15 million dollar receivables facility with Bridgin. The pattern is the same as Apollo's. Equity pays for the product, and the assets are funded against the cash they generate.

**Three of these five cases involve the same small structuring firm.** Kaleidofin built both Apollo's securitisation and SunCulture's, and both were described as firsts of their kind in their market. That detail says something the funding totals do not. The constraint was never only how much money existed. Part of it was that almost nobody could build the instrument the balance sheet needed, so the balance sheet took the instrument that was already on the shelf.

That matrix is a judgement, not a measurement, and it is published with its reasoning attached so the judgement can be argued with. What it shows is the matching problem in one frame. The venture capital column has exactly one strong cell, and it sits on the row furthest from what a farmer actually earns. The money that is easiest to raise fits the part of the sector that matters least for the outcome everyone says they want.

## What a mismatch actually does to a company

The wrong instrument does not sit quietly on the balance sheet. It changes behaviour, and the pattern is predictable enough to be worth naming.

The company chases growth its margin cannot fund, because the equity story requires a steep line. It raises again too early, because the runway was priced for a trajectory the business does not have. It expands into new countries to show a bigger market rather than deepening the one relationship that was working. It builds an expensive direct channel to farmers instead of plugging into the channel that already reaches them. Then the equity market turns, and the company discovers that nothing in the business was ever going to produce the exit its shareholders were promised.

A large share of what gets written up as agricultural technology failure in Africa is this. Not a failure of the product, the market or the founder, but a company that took the only cheque on offer and then had to spend years pretending to be a different kind of company.

Twiga is the clearest illustration, and it is worth being precise about what it does and does not prove. Twiga expanded into new categories, bought distributors, built its own logistics estate and leased a hub that insiders later described as a financial millstone. Each of those is a defensible decision for a company that has to show a steep line. None of them fits a produce distribution business funded by trade finance against its own stock. The company has not said the instrument was the cause, and plenty else went wrong. But the direction of its 2025 restructuring, which was explicitly toward an asset light model, is the clearest evidence available that the shape of the business and the shape of the money had come apart.

The people tracking the sector describe the same mechanism. Briter puts the equity retreat down to what these businesses own. Lorries, warehouses full of seed and fertiliser, loan books owed by farmers. Each needs cash up front, and none of it is what equity is good at funding. Briter also notes how the commercial investors who did come back came back: not by leading equity rounds, but in structured deals alongside development finance institutions. That is not a market losing interest. That is a market changing instrument.

## The measure that quietly defeats the purpose

There is a second mismatch, and it sits inside development finance rather than inside the companies. It concerns how programmes are judged.

Leverage is simple to state, and it is worth being exact about it because the word gets used loosely. A programme like Aceli spends donor money on two things: cash paid to a lender for each qualifying loan it originates, and cover against part of the losses if those loans go bad. The loans themselves are funded by the lender, out of its own deposits and capital. Leverage is the ratio between the two. If Aceli spends 1 million dollars of donor money and the banks consequently put 10 million dollars of their own money out of the door as loans, that is 10x.

So "moving commercial money" is not a metaphor. It means the dollars came off a commercial bank's balance sheet rather than a donor's, and they have to be repaid to that bank rather than granted. More of that per donor dollar looks like better value, and as a rough test of efficiency it is reasonable.

It is also completely silent on the only question that matters, which is who got the loans.

The two measures are near mirror images. In the smallest band, 84 per cent of borrowers had never held a formal loan, and every dollar of subsidy brought about 5 dollars off the lenders' own balance sheets. In the largest band, 8 per cent were first time borrowers and every dollar of subsidy brought 29.

That is not a coincidence, and the mechanism is not mysterious. A business that can absorb a 1.5 million dollar loan has accounts, assets and probably a bank already. The donor dollar riding alongside it is not changing very much. A manager told to maximise leverage therefore has one reliable strategy, which is to write bigger cheques. Pushed far enough, that incentive produces a 29x ratio while the programme finances companies that had other options. The number on the slide looks excellent. The programme has stopped doing its job.

That reframes a set of results that looks, at first glance, like underperformance.

Aceli missed two of its targets. It mobilised 440 million dollars, not 600. It reached 10x leverage, not 15x. On everything else it overshot, and not by a little. It made 5,576 loans where it had projected 1,500. It reached 2.3 million farmers and workers where it had projected 1.1 million. And 63 per cent of its borrowers had never held a formal loan, against a projection of 35 per cent.

Those are not separate results. The misses and the overshoots are the same sentence. Aceli mobilised less money and generated less leverage because it went much further down market than planned, with an average loan of 79,000 dollars against a projected 400,000. Its 10x is still more than double the roughly 4x average across blended finance globally. It outperformed the field while serving harder borrowers, and still looked like a miss against its own forecast.

## The borrowers who get the least money are not the riskiest

The same dataset tests an assumption the industry rarely examines, and the assumption does not survive contact with it.

Businesses owned by women default on 3.3 per cent of loans. Businesses owned by young entrepreneurs default on 2.5 per cent. Businesses owned by men over 35 default on 4.6 per cent. The group the market treats as marginal is the safer borrower, by a clear margin.

They also borrow about half as much: an average of 49,000 and 36,000 dollars against 95,000. That is where the trap closes. Smaller loans produce less leverage, so a programme measured on leverage is pushed away from exactly these borrowers without anyone ever deciding to exclude them. The bias is in the arithmetic, not in the room.

One caution, because the data is narrow. These figures describe one programme in five countries. They do not establish that women and young entrepreneurs are safer borrowers everywhere, and a single portfolio can reflect how that portfolio was selected as much as how borrowers behave. What the figures do remove is the excuse that smaller loans to underserved groups must be worse credit. Aceli says it knows of no other loan performance data at this scale anywhere in the world broken down by sex or age. The sector has been allocating capital on an assumption it has almost never tested.

## What actually makes these businesses financeable

If the binding constraint is the cost of reaching a borrower, then viability comes from attacking that cost. Four approaches do, and a fifth fixes a different problem that is just as expensive.

**Finance the group, not the individual.** A dairy cooperative with 400 members is one credit file. Four hundred farmers are four hundred. So assess the cooperative, the outgrower scheme or the buyer's supplier list. One assessment then covers many borrowers, and this is the largest single saving available.

**Build the loan into a sale that is happening anyway.** The input seller already has the farmer in the shop. The crop buyer already has the lorry at the gate. Lend at that moment and the cost of finding the customer has already been paid by somebody else.

**Replace the field visit with data that predicts repayment just as well.** What the buyer paid the farmer last season. What the mobile money account shows. What sits in the warehouse. What the satellite says the yield will be. Each one takes a line out of the cost of assessment.

**Price the book, not the name.** Losses turned out to be flat across loan sizes, between 0.7 and 1.1 per cent. Flat and predictable is exactly the condition under which a lender can price a whole book at once. It is also the condition under which assessing every borrower from scratch is waste.

**And lend in the currency the business earns.** A trader who earns shillings and repays dollars is carrying a bet on the exchange rate, and nobody is paying them to carry it. Research by TCX suggests that in any given year, one in eight low income countries sees its currency fall by a fifth or more.

Uganda spent most of 2026 being that one in eight. The shilling went from about 3,604 to the dollar in March to nearly 4,500 by late October. A business that borrowed dollars in March and was repaying in October watched the debt grow by roughly a quarter in shilling terms, without borrowing another cent. The maize it was buying and the grain it was selling were priced in shillings throughout. Nothing about the business changed. The loan simply got bigger.

Then the second blow landed, and it came from the same event. On 14 October the Bank of Uganda raised its policy rate from 9.75 per cent to 12 per cent to defend the currency. It also lifted the share of deposits banks must hold in reserve, from 11 per cent to 13.5 per cent. For a farm borrower that is three things at once. Dollar debt costs more to service. Shilling debt costs more to take on. And banks have less to lend, because more of every deposit is now parked at the central bank rather than working. None of it had anything to do with farming.

That last point explains a shift that deserves more attention than it gets. Aceli expected international impact investors to do about half the lending. They did almost none of it. Of its 55 partner lenders, 45 are African institutions, and they made 96 per cent of the loans, in local currency, to businesses offshore investors could not reach.

The same substitution has been running through African venture capital, traced in [Who Is Actually Writing the Cheques](https://herufi.org/blogs/who-writes-the-cheques-african-vc). In agriculture it matters more. A local bank has two things offshore capital cannot manufacture: branches near the farms, and deposits in the currency the farms earn.

---

## So is the equity retreat a failure?

Partly, and mostly not.

Some genuinely good companies were starved when the only instrument on offer stopped being offered, and that is a real cost borne by real founders. But most of what happened was a correction, and an overdue one. Money is migrating toward instruments these balance sheets can actually service, which is what should have happened years earlier.

The danger now is the opposite mistake. The sector spent a decade offering equity to businesses that needed working capital. It could spend the next decade offering working capital to businesses that need patient long term money for irrigation, storage and processing. That is the 27 billion dollar layer that is 99.6 per cent unserved, and no revolving trade line will ever reach it. Fixing one mismatch by installing another is not progress, it is a different mistake with better press.

Which leaves the question a donor actually has to answer. If the constraint is cost, and different businesses need different instruments, what would change if the money were spent that way?

That is what the final article tests.
