I built this model to prove something, and it refused.

The first two articles in this series established that small farm loans fail on cost rather than risk, and that different agricultural businesses need different instruments. The obvious next step was to show that a donor spending a fixed budget on the cost of lending, rather than on covering credit losses, would produce more lending. I expected a clean result. I got a flat one: holding the budget constant and switching what it buys moves total lending by less than 1 per cent, and in the wrong direction.

That null result turned out to be more useful than the finding I went looking for. The reason behind it is structural rather than accidental, and it points at something the sector systematically fails to measure.

**How much concessional money exists decides how much lending happens. What that money is spent on decides who gets the loan.** Those are two different questions, and almost every programme reports only the first.

Before any of the numbers, one thing needs saying clearly, because it governs how everything below should be read.

| What it is | How to treat it |
|---|---|
| Lending economics from 32,000 real loans | Observed data, published by Aceli Africa |
| The relationship between loan size and cost | Estimated, fitted to five published points |
| How strongly lenders shift their book when margins change | An assumption, calibrated on one observation |
| Scenario results in dollars | Model output under stated assumptions, not forecasts |
| A 30 per cent fall in the cost of lending | An assumption to be tested, not a measurement |

Nothing below is a prediction about what African agricultural lending will do. It is a comparison between five versions of the present, which is a more modest and far more answerable question.

---

## What the model does, in plain terms

The model copies one decision: a bank choosing where to put its money. The bank can lend to agriculture, buy government bonds, or lend elsewhere. It compares what each option earns and allocates accordingly.

For every loan size, it works out what the lender actually keeps. Start with interest and fees. Subtract the cost of finding and managing the borrower. Subtract expected losses, less whatever a guarantee covers. Subtract the cost of the bank's own funding. What remains is the margin.

Then it asks how much of the bank's book flows to agriculture at that margin. Banks do not abandon farming entirely when it pays less than treasury bills, and they do not pour everything in when it pays more. They move at the edges. The model is calibrated so that it reproduces the one real world number available, which is agriculture sitting at about 4 per cent of these banks' loan books.

That calibration is also the model's weakest point, and I return to it below rather than burying it.

---

## Five futures

**Constrained.** Donors pull back. Guarantees shrink, support for the cost of lending disappears, funding gets dearer and government bonds pay more. Agriculture looks worse on every dimension at once.

**Baseline.** Roughly today. Partial guarantees are common and modest cost support exists in the few markets that have built it.

**Favourable.** More donor money arrives, and it is spent the way donor money is usually spent, which is overwhelmingly on covering credit losses.

**Matched.** Exactly the same donor budget as Favourable, down to the decimal place, facing exactly the same market. The only difference is the split. Loss cover drops back to today's level and every freed shilling goes into paying for the cost of finding and assessing borrowers.

**Matched plus a cheaper way of lending.** The same budget again, plus one assumption: lenders cut the cost of making a loan by 30 per cent, by sharing agent networks, using buyers' transaction records, assessing portfolios rather than individuals and moving money digitally.

Scenarios three and four are the heart of it. They cost the same, by construction, so if they produce different results the difference is the mechanism and nothing else. Scenario five then asks a separate question: what if the work itself became cheaper, rather than merely being subsidised?

---

## Spending the same money differently barely changes the total

Favourable produces 4.10 billion dollars of lending. Matched produces 4.07 billion. The difference is under 1 per cent, and it is in the wrong direction.

The reason is worth understanding, because it is not a flaw in the argument. Half of all agricultural loan value sits in loans above 200,000 dollars. In that range the cost of making the loan is already tiny, between 0.9 and 2.5 per cent, so paying it down achieves very little. A guarantee there still returns a full percentage point of expected loss, so on large loans it is simply the better gift.

Large loans dominate the total, which makes the total a poor test of anything. If you only ever look at how many dollars flowed into agriculture, you cannot see whether the programme reached anybody it had not reached before.

So the honest conclusion is narrower than the one I set out to prove, and considerably more useful. The amount of concessional money determines the volume of lending. It is close to indifferent to how that money is spent.

Which means the sector has been measuring the wrong thing. Ask the other question.

---

## It changes who gets the loan

Split the same lending by loan size and the scenarios finally separate.

| Scenario | Total lending | Loans of 10k to 50k | Lending to first time borrowers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Constrained | 2.03bn | 0.18bn | 0.45bn |
| Baseline | 2.93bn | 0.27bn | 0.66bn |
| Favourable | 4.10bn | 0.74bn | 1.17bn |
| Matched | 4.07bn | 0.74bn | 1.17bn |
| Matched plus a cheaper way of lending | 4.41bn | 0.96bn | 1.38bn |

*Model output. Counts only loan sizes earning a positive margin, on the view that banks do not build a book in a product that loses money. The last column weights lending by how likely borrowers in that size band are to be borrowing formally for the first time.*

Between today and the last scenario, total lending rises 51 per cent. Lending in the smallest loan sizes rises 252 per cent. Those small loans are where 84 per cent of borrowers have never held a formal loan, so lending that reaches genuinely new borrowers roughly doubles.

That is the finding. More money moves the total. Better targeted money moves the frontier.

## Half a percentage point is not a business

One row in the model matters more than any total, and it is the smallest loans, because that is where the first time borrowers are.

| Scenario | What the bank keeps on a loan of 10,000 to 25,000 dollars |
|---|---|
| Constrained | -3.52% |
| Baseline | -0.93% |
| Favourable | +0.53% |
| Matched | +0.55% |
| Matched plus a cheaper way of lending | +4.65% |

In today's conditions these loans lose money, so they largely do not get made. Under the two better funded scenarios they cross into profit. Look closely at how they cross.

Half a percentage point is not a margin. It is a rounding error with ambitions. One poor season erases it. A small rise in funding costs erases it. And it still has to be defended in a meeting where the treasury desk is earning 16 per cent on government paper for no work at all.

Only the cheaper operating model produces a margin a bank will actually build around. At 4.65 per cent somebody can staff a department, write a policy and defend the budget when it is questioned.

This is the distinction the usual debate misses entirely. A subsidy can lift a loan category across zero. Only a lower cost structure keeps it there. Technically profitable and durably profitable are not the same thing, and only the second survives contact with a bad year.

It is also why the model does not recommend abandoning guarantees. On larger loans they do useful work, and the chart above shows it. The argument is specific: when the goal is reaching businesses the market has never served, the cost of making the loan deserves far more attention than it currently gets.

## What a cheaper way of lending would actually involve

The 30 per cent cost reduction is an assumption, so it is worth being concrete about what would have to happen for it to be real.

**Reach borrowers through networks that already exist.** A crop buyer deals with 300 suppliers and knows which of them deliver. Work through that buyer, or through a cooperative, a processor or an input seller, rather than finding 300 businesses one at a time. One relationship opens many doors, and the partner already knows who is reliable.

**Use the trading records instead of starting from scratch.** A crop buyer knows how much a supplier delivers, how often, and whether the quality holds. Where those records are good they can replace work a loan officer would otherwise do from nothing.

**Assess the book, not only the borrower.** A lender with hundreds of similar businesses on its books can study how the whole book behaves, while still running sensible checks on individuals. That cuts out repeated work, and it is what makes a large number of small loans manageable.

**Move the money digitally.** Paying out and collecting through a phone cuts the clerical work of handling cash and tracking who has paid. How much it saves depends on the network, the quality of the records, and whether borrowers can actually use the system.

These reinforce one another, which is the point. A lender working through a buyer network also gets the transaction data. That data makes portfolio assessment possible. Digital payments then make collection cheap. The saving comes from redesigning the whole process, not from shaving each step.

None of this is proven. A shared agent network might cut costs in Kenya and raise them in Zambia. Transaction records might work for established suppliers and fail for new ones. The model gives a reason to test these approaches. It does not show they work everywhere.

## What happens if donors leave, and the question that hides

The constrained scenario is not hypothetical. Development budgets are under pressure across the board, and the model says what that would cost: lending falls from 2.93 billion dollars to 2.03 billion, a drop of about 31 per cent. The smallest loan category, already loss making today at minus 0.93 per cent, goes to minus 3.52. It moves from marginally out of reach to nowhere near.

A reader of the draft asked the right question about that scenario. Does it mean agricultural lending only works when economic conditions are good?

As built, the scenario cannot answer that, because it changes two things at the same time. Donors retrench, so guarantee cover thins and the origination support disappears. The economy also deteriorates, so funding costs rise, government bonds pay more and loan sizes drift smaller. Bundling them is realistic, since budget pressure and macro stress tend to arrive together. It also makes the result impossible to attribute. So I ran each half on its own.

| What changes | Lending | Against today |
|---|---|---|
| Today | 2.93bn | |
| Donors retrench, economy holds steady | 2.80bn | **-4.5%** |
| Economy deteriorates, donors hold steady | 2.15bn | **-26.4%** |
| Both, which is the Constrained scenario | 2.03bn | **-30.5%** |

*Model output. The two halves do not sum exactly to the whole, because the allocation rule is a curve rather than a line. The gap between them is small, about 0.01 billion dollars.*

So the answer to the question is yes, and more emphatically than I expected. Roughly six sevenths of the damage in the worst case comes from the economy, not from the aid budget. Donors withdrawing entirely from what they currently do costs about 4.5 per cent of lending. A worse macro environment costs about 26 per cent.

That is uncomfortable for anyone reading this series as an argument purely about how donors spend, and it should be. It is also the first article's point arriving with a bill attached. When the risk free alternative improves, agriculture loses, whatever donors do. Uganda's policy rate went from 9.75 to 12 per cent in a single October meeting. That one decision moves the bar for every farm loan in the country by more than the entire origination support programme described in this series could move it back.

Two conclusions follow, and they point the same way.

The first is that the case for attacking the cost of lending gets stronger, not weaker. If the macro environment does most of the damage and nobody in agricultural finance controls it, then the cost base is the main lever anyone in this sector actually holds. A system that only works while donors pay for every transaction is a system with an expiry date, and a system that only works while treasury bills are cheap has a shorter one.

The second is a warning about how this research should be used. A programme that lowers the cost of lending by 30 per cent, and then meets a year like Uganda's 2026, will show disappointing numbers. The programme will not have failed. The denominator moved. Anyone evaluating this kind of intervention needs to say in advance what macro conditions they are assuming, or they will end up measuring the bond market and calling it a programme evaluation.

But the model does not show that all agricultural lending can become commercially self sustaining, and I would not want it read that way. Some lending will still need loss cover. Some investments will still need patient money. Some activities produce public benefits that no commercial lender can capture, and those will always need a grant.

The aim is not to abolish concessional finance. It is to spend it where it changes the outcome, rather than spending it forever to compensate for inefficiency that could have been designed out.

## How much to trust any of this

A model can look precise without being certain, so here is where this one is weak.

The lending economics come from one programme. Aceli's data covers 2020 to 2023 in five countries with relatively developed banking systems. West and Central Africa are probably harder, not easier, which would strengthen the direction of the argument while making the levels wrong.

The cost curve rests on five observations. The relationship between loan size and cost fits those five points almost perfectly, but five points cannot describe every market, and a near perfect fit on five points is a warning as much as a reassurance.

The way banks respond to margins is calibrated on a single real world number. One observation fixes one parameter and tells you nothing about the shape of the curve anywhere else.

That last one is the weakest link, so I tested whether it matters.

Running the model across a fourfold range for that parameter, the dollar figures move by a factor of about seven between the extremes. The ranking of the five scenarios does not change at all. The lines never cross. Matched with a cheaper operating model stays ahead of Favourable, which stays ahead of Matched, which stays ahead of Baseline, which stays ahead of Constrained.

Treat the ordering as the finding and the dollar amounts as illustration.

The 30 per cent cost saving is an assumption rather than a measurement. If the true figure were 15 per cent, the final scenario would lose roughly half its advantage. It would not change the ranking.

And the model only looks at one side of the market. It estimates how lenders respond to economics. It says nothing about whether enough suitable businesses exist to borrow, whether they can use more credit productively, or what happens to prices when credit expands.

That last limitation is the one I would worry about most. A programme can make lending profitable for banks and still miss its targets because there are not enough businesses ready to borrow. Aceli mobilised 73 per cent of its capital target, which suggests pipeline is a live constraint rather than a theoretical one.

## What would prove this wrong

The argument is testable, and four things would undo it.

Credit losses could turn out to rise sharply as loans get smaller, in a bigger or differently selected sample. Risk would then matter more than this analysis says, and the case for guarantees would strengthen considerably.

A guarantee only programme could successfully expand lending to the smallest businesses with no cost support at all. The arithmetic in the first article says it cannot, and a clear counterexample would break the whole chain.

Attempts to cut the cost of lending could fail in practice. Suppose agent networks, transaction data, portfolio assessment and digital payments simply do not lower costs in the real world. Then the final scenario is fiction, and the honest conclusion is that small loans need permanent subsidy. That would be a finding too, and a considerably more uncomfortable one.

Or agricultural technology equity returns could recover sharply, which would suggest the retreat from 2022 to 2025 was a cycle rather than a correction.

The third test is the one that matters, because the entire recommendation rests on it. The sector needs to know whether lenders can cut the cost of reaching borrowers without quietly weakening credit assessment, which would move the problem rather than solve it.

A pilot would settle it. Take two groups of similar agricultural businesses. Finance one through a lender's existing process. Finance the other through a model built on buyer relationships, transaction data, portfolio assessment and digital collection. Then measure cost per loan, time to disburse, repayment performance, how many borrowers were new, and how much subsidy each dollar lent required. If costs fall and performance holds, scale it. If not, the results show which parts of the process are stubbornly expensive, which is worth knowing either way. Either outcome moves the central assumption out of a spreadsheet and into evidence.

## Who should do what

The division of labour that follows from all this is not the one currently running.

**Local banks are the only institutions that can reach the smallest businesses.** They have the branches, the local currency deposits and the existing cost base. What stops them is a 16.4 per cent cost of making the loan, not a shortage of nerve. Aceli's 45 African lenders made 96 per cent of its loans, against a plan that assumed international investors would do half.

**Donors are the only source of money that can pay for the cost of lending.** That cost generates no repayment, so no instrument other than a grant can fund it. This is the highest value use of a shrinking budget, and it is not where most of the budget goes.

**Development finance institutions should fund balance sheets, not borrowers.** Checking a 2 million dollar deal costs roughly what checking a 20 million dollar deal costs. That is why their minimum cheque is arithmetic rather than preference. Their advantage lies upstream: lending to local banks, guaranteeing portfolios, supplying local currency for longer than anyone else will.

**Impact and patient investors should fund two things.** The platforms that make lending cheaper, and the long term assets that are 99.6 per cent unserved: irrigation, storage, processing, machinery. That layer needs money with a longer life than a seven year fund.

**Venture capital should fund the one business model that has venture economics** and stop underwriting crop processors as though they were software companies. That is a smaller market than the sector has told itself. It is also an honest one, and an honest small market is a better place to work than a large imaginary one.

**And everyone should report leverage next to additionality, always.** A leverage ratio with no measure of who was reached is not a result. It is a selection effect with a number attached.

---

## The sentence it reduces to

Africa's agricultural finance problem is not mainly a shortage of money. Debt has risen to 41 per cent of all African technology capital. Local banks have stepped into gaps international investors could not reach. Capital has been moving, and moving quickly.

It has been moving toward the instruments these balance sheets can service, which is progress, and toward the borrowers who were nearly bankable already, which is not.

So the work now has two parts. Match the right capital to the right business at the right stage of growth. Then pay for the one thing standing between a local bank and a farmer who has never held a formal loan.

That thing is the cost of making the loan. On a 15,000 dollar loan it is 16.4 per cent. Everything else is downstream of it.
